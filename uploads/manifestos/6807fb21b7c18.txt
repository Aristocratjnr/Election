Abstract

This paper explores the origins and evolution of computer ethics, tracing its development from early theoretical discussions during World War II to its current role in addressing the complex ethical challenges posed by modern computing technologies. The study begins by examining the foundational contributions of pioneers such as Norbert Wiener and Joseph Weizenbaum, whose work in the mid-20th century raised early concerns about automation, dehumanization, and the moral responsibilities of machines. It then details the institutionalization of the field in the 1970s and 1980s, highlighting the contributions of scholars like Walter Maner and Deborah Johnson, as well as the integration of ethical standards by professional bodies such as the Association for Computing Machinery.
